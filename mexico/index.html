Buying Property in Mexico as a Foreigner

The restricted zone, the fideicomiso, and what a Notario is really for. Written for a first-time foreign buyer.

General guidance only. This page explains how the process usually works so you can ask better questions. It is not legal, tax or immigration advice, and it is not a recommendation of any firm or person. Rules and rates change, often annually — every figure below is dated and sourced, and you should confirm the current position with the official authority or a qualified professional before you commit money.

Who can own property in Mexico, and under what structure

Foreigners can own property anywhere in Mexico. The complication is how you hold it, and it depends entirely on where the property sits.

Mexico's constitution defines a restricted zone: land within 50 kilometres of any coastline and within 100 kilometres of any land border. Outside that zone, a foreign buyer takes direct title exactly as a Mexican national would. Inside it — which covers most of the places foreign buyers actually want, from Puerto Vallarta to Playa del Carmen — a foreigner cannot hold direct title and must use one of two structures:

  • A fideicomiso (bank trust). A Mexican bank holds legal title as trustee, and you are the named beneficiary. This is not a lease and not a diluted form of ownership: as beneficiary you have the full right to occupy, renovate, rent out, sell, and leave the property to your heirs. The trust runs for a 50-year term and is renewable. This is the normal route for a residential buyer.
  • A Mexican corporation. Used where the property is genuinely commercial or where there are several properties. It carries ongoing accounting and tax filing obligations, and using one purely to hold a holiday home is usually more cost and complexity than it is worth. Take advice specific to your circumstances before choosing this over a trust.

A great deal of nervous folklore surrounds the fideicomiso. It is a routine, decades-old instrument, and the bank cannot sell or encumber your property — its role is custodial and it acts on your instruction.

The professionals you actually need

A Notario Público

This is the role most misunderstood by American, Canadian and British buyers, because the word "notary" means something far smaller in their own countries. A Mexican Notario Público is a government-appointed attorney, admitted in limited numbers, who holds public office. Every property transfer must pass through one. They draft and execute the deed, calculate and withhold the taxes due, and register the transfer.

Crucially, the Notario is a neutral public official, not your advocate. They confirm the transaction is lawful and properly recorded. They are not retained to negotiate for you, to hunt for problems on your behalf, or to advise you commercially. Buyers who treat the Notario as their lawyer are effectively going through the purchase unrepresented.

An independent real estate attorney

Separate from the Notario, and the person actually working for you. They verify the seller's title in the Public Registry of Property, check for liens, unpaid property tax (predial) and utility debts, confirm the boundaries and permits, and review the trust deed before you sign.

The trustee bank, and an appraiser

If you are buying in the restricted zone, a Mexican bank must act as trustee, and you should understand its setup and annual fees before choosing. A formal appraisal (avalúo) is generally required and feeds into the tax calculation.

The real cost stack

Closing costs in Mexico fall mostly on the buyer. Budget 5%–8% of the price outside the restricted zone, and 7%–12% where a fideicomiso is required, as at 2026.

Residency routes

Owning property in Mexico does not by itself give you residency, and residency is not needed to buy. The usual routes are temporary residency (renewable, converting to permanent after four years) and permanent residency applied for directly, both assessed on income or savings.

As at 2026 the commonly cited benchmarks are roughly US$4,400 per month for temporary and US$7,400 per month for permanent residency — but these are genuinely approximate. The thresholds are defined in Mexican minimum-wage units, each consulate applies its own conversion, and the dollar figure moves with the exchange rate. Treat the numbers as a planning range and confirm with the specific consulate you will apply through, since applications are normally started outside Mexico.

Three mistakes that cost newcomers real money

Buying ejido landEjido land is communally held under a separate legal regime and generally cannot be validly sold to a foreign buyer at all. It is offered cheaply and regularly, and buyers have lost their entire investment. If a coastal or rural plot looks far too cheap, this is very often why. Your attorney must confirm the land is fully titled and registered.
Treating the Notario as your lawyerThey are a neutral public official. Nobody is checking the deal in your interest unless you retain your own attorney, and by the time a problem surfaces at signing it is usually too late to renegotiate.
Forgetting the fideicomiso runs an annual billThe setup cost is visible at closing and the yearly trustee fee is not. Several hundred dollars a year, indefinitely, materially changes the running cost of a modest holiday property.
