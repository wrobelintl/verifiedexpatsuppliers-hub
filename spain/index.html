Buying property in Spain as a foreigner

A free, sourced guide to the process, written for a first-time foreign buyer. Every figure is dated and linked to where it came from.

Who can own property in Spain, and under what structure

There is no restriction on foreign ownership of Spanish property. A buyer of any nationality can hold Spanish real estate outright, in their own name, with the same freehold title a Spanish national would get. You do not need residency, a Spanish company, or a trust structure. The one document you cannot proceed without is an NIE (Número de Identificación de Extranjero) — a foreigner's tax identification number. Every buyer named on the deed needs their own. You can apply through a Spanish consulate in your home country or in Spain, and it is the single most common cause of a delayed completion, so start it early.

One change matters enormously if you have been reading older guides: buying property in Spain no longer grants you residency. Spain's golden visa, which gave residence rights in exchange for a €500,000 property purchase, was abolished on 3 April 2025. People who applied before that date keep their rights, but the route is closed to new applicants. If someone is still selling you Spanish property on the basis that it comes with a visa, that is a reason to walk away from that adviser.

The professionals you actually need

An independent lawyer (abogado)

This is the one that saves people the most money, and the one most often got wrong. Your lawyer must be independent — not the developer's lawyer, not the estate agent's recommendation, not someone whose fee depends on the sale completing. Their job is to obtain the nota simple from the Land Registry (Registro de la Propiedad) and confirm who actually owns the property, whether there is an outstanding mortgage or embargo against it, whether unpaid community fees or local taxes will transfer to you, and whether what has been built matches what has planning permission. Illegal or partly-unlicensed builds are common in rural and coastal Spain and are very expensive to discover after completion.

A notary (notario)

The notary is a public official, not your representative. They witness and execute the deed (escritura pública) and confirm both parties understood it. They do not act in your interest and will not chase the due diligence your lawyer should be doing. Their fees are set by state tariff, so there is little point shopping around.

A tax adviser (asesor fiscal or gestor)

Needed after you buy, not before. If you are a non-resident owner you have an annual filing obligation whether or not you rent the property out — see below. Many owners do not discover this for years, and the back-filing plus surcharges is an unpleasant surprise.

A surveyor or architect, and a currency broker

A structural survey is not customary in Spain the way it is in the UK, but on an older, rural or self-built property it is worth commissioning one. Separately, if you are converting a large sum from sterling or dollars, compare a specialist currency broker against your bank's rate — on a €300,000 purchase the spread between the two is routinely worth more than your legal fees.

Then there are the costs that continue every year, which buyers routinely leave out of their budget:

  • IBI — the annual municipal property tax, set by your town hall against the property's cadastral value.
  • IRNR (non-resident income tax) — if you are not a Spanish tax resident, Spain taxes you on a deemed rental income even when the property sits empty all year. The taxable amount is 1.1% of the cadastral value (or 2% where the value has not been revised in the last ten tax periods), taxed at 19% for EU/EEA residents and 24% for everyone else, filed on Modelo 210. Annually if vacant, quarterly if let.
  • Community fees on any property in a block or development, plus utilities standing charges.

Residency routes, now that the golden visa has gone

EU, EEA and Swiss citizens have freedom of movement and simply register. For everyone else, the two routes most property buyers use are:

  • Non-lucrative visa (NLV) — for people who can support themselves without working. Requires passive income of 400% of the IPREM index, which for 2026 means roughly €2,400 per month for the main applicant plus about €600 per month per dependant. Critically, it prohibits all work, and most consulates read that as including remote work for a foreign employer.
  • Digital nomad visa — introduced under the 2023 startups law, for remote workers with non-Spanish employers or clients. Requires roughly €2,849 per month in 2026, calculated from the Spanish minimum wage rather than the IPREM, and allows no more than 20% of income from Spanish sources.

The IPREM is reset annually, so the non-lucrative threshold moves each year. Check the figure for the year you actually apply.

Three mistakes that cost newcomers real money

Using the lawyer the agent or developer recommendedTheir incentive is completion, not your protection. This is the single most expensive mistake in Spanish property, and it is entirely avoidable. Appoint your own, before you pay any reservation deposit.
Budgeting the purchase price and nothing elseTen to fifteen per cent on top is not an edge case, it is the normal outcome. Buyers who have committed every last euro to the price find themselves unable to complete, and reservation deposits are usually non-refundable.
Not filing Modelo 210 because the property is emptyThe obligation exists whether or not you earn a cent from the property. It goes unnoticed for years and then surfaces with interest and surcharges attached, often when you come to sell.
