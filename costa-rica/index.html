Buying property in Costa Rica as a foreigner

A free, sourced guide to the process, written for a first-time foreign buyer. Every figure is dated and linked to where it came from.

Who can own property in Costa Rica, and where the exception is

Costa Rica has some of the most straightforward foreign ownership rules in the Americas. A foreigner has the same property rights as a Costa Rican citizen: you can hold titled land outright in your own name, with no residency requirement, no trust structure, and no local partner. For the great majority of properties, that is the whole story.

The exception is the coast, and it catches people out badly because it applies to exactly the beachfront land many buyers come for. Costa Rica's Maritime Terrestrial Zone (Zona Marítimo Terrestre, or ZMT) covers the first 200 metres inland from the high-tide line along virtually the entire coast, and it is divided in two:

  • The first 50 metres is public. It cannot be owned, leased or built on by anyone, private individual or company. It is protected for public use.
  • The next 150 metres is the restricted zone. Land here is not sold freehold — it is granted by concession from the local municipality, typically for terms of 5 to 20 years, renewable. And there are two rules that specifically affect foreign buyers: a foreigner may hold no more than 49% of a concession, and to hold one at all a foreigner must have been a legal resident of Costa Rica for at least five years.

So a concession is a fundamentally different and weaker thing than titled land, and it is sometimes marketed to foreign buyers in language that blurs the two. Titled beach-area property does exist — typically on bluffs, hillsides and back-beach lots that fall legally outside the 200-metre zone — and can be owned outright by a foreigner with no residency requirement at all. Establishing which of the two you are being offered is the first question to answer about any coastal property in Costa Rica, before price is even discussed.

The professionals you actually need

An attorney, who is also the notary

Costa Rica merges two roles that Spain and Mexico keep separate: attorneys here are also notaries, and a notario público executes and registers the transfer. That efficiency comes with a caution. If the same professional is acting for both sides of the transaction, or was introduced by the seller's agent, you do not have independent representation. Retain your own.

Their substantive work is verifying title in the Registro Nacional — Costa Rica's national registry is public, searchable and unusually good, which makes proper due diligence very achievable. They should confirm the registered owner, the plot boundaries against the plano catastrado (cadastral survey plan), any liens, mortgages or annotations, whether the property is titled or concession, and any water availability or environmental restriction.

Escrow, and a surveyor

Using a registered escrow agent to hold funds is standard practice and strongly advisable. A surveyor confirming the physical boundaries match the registered plan is worth the cost, particularly on rural and larger plots where discrepancies are common.

Residency routes

Buying property does not itself grant residency, though the investor route below is property-friendly. The established categories are:

  • Pensionado — proof of at least US$1,000 per month in lifetime pension or retirement income.
  • Rentista — proof of at least US$2,500 per month from a guaranteed source, evidenced for two years. This must be genuine yield such as dividends, interest or rental income, documented with annual statements.
  • Inversionista — an investment of US$150,000 or more in real estate or a business.
  • Digital nomad — foreign-source income of US$3,000 or more per month.

Temporary residency is generally granted for two years and renewed on a two-year cycle, with a path to permanent residency after three years and to citizenship after seven years of legal residence.

Three mistakes that cost newcomers real money

Buying a concession while believing you bought landInside the 150-metre restricted zone you are acquiring a time-limited municipal concession, capped at 49% foreign ownership and requiring five years' prior legal residency. It is not freehold title and should not be priced as though it were.
Letting one attorney act for both sidesBecause attorneys are also notaries here, it is easy to end up with the seller's professional handling "the paperwork" and to mistake that for having representation. Appoint your own.
Missing 15 January in your first year of ownershipAnnual property tax and the luxury home declaration both fall due then, and the luxury threshold moves every January. Surcharges and penalties follow a missed filing.
